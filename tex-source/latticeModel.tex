\metatitle{Lattice models}
\metadescription{Brief overview of what a lattice model is}

\section[latticeModel]{Introduction}

From \cite{Karhadkar2022x}: A \defin{lattice model} is based on an edge-labeled graph 
(usually a rectangular grid) with some local constraints. 
Each vertex is assigned a weight, depending on its adjacent edges. 
If every vertex satisfies the constraints, we say that this assignment is an \defin{admissible state}.
The goal is to conclude some sort of global behavior.

The \defin{partition function} of a lattice model, is the sum over all admissible states:
\[
 Z = \sum_{S \in \mathrm{ states}} \prod_{v \in V} \mathrm{weight}_S(v).
\]

Depending on the conditions, various functions and identities may be recovered.
For example, we can obtain the \hyperref[schurS]{Schur functions},
the \hyperref[schurCauchyFormula]{dual Cauchy identity}, etc.

From now on, we assume that we are in the rectangular grid 
setting (each vertex has four adjacent neighbors) unless stated otherwise.

Each such state is then assigned a weight, so in the $n$-vertex model, 
there are $n$ possible weights associated with a vertex.
This weight is sometimes referred to as the \defin{Boltzmann weight},
and the matrix which records the weights is called the \defin{R-matrix}.

It is usually interesting to compute the partition function 
with some fixed \defin{boundary condition} or \defin{wall condition}
that is, labels on the edges \enquote{exiting} the rectangular grid.


\subsection[YangBaxter]{The Yang--Baxter equation}

In order to discuss the Yang--Baxter equation,
the edges in the graph can be seen as forming strands, or braidings.

The \defin{Yang--Baxter equation} is a relation that describes how
certain quantities are preserved as strands are permuted.
This is useful for proving symmetries.

Models which satisfy the Yang--Baxter equation are 
called \defin{integrable} or \defin{exactly solvable}. 

For the Schur vertex model, \name{Leonid Petrov} shows that each local
Yang--Baxter identity has a unique weight-preserving matching of its summands
\cite[Lem.~3.2]{Petrov2026x}.  Moving this matching across a grid produces
Fomin's growth-diagram form of \hyperref[rskYangBaxter]{RSK}
\cite[Prop.~3.5]{Petrov2026x}.  The same local rule is a three-dimensional
$R$ satisfying the tetrahedron equation; its periodic closure gives the
combinatorial $R$-matrices of one-row crystals.  For deformed Schur weights,
probabilistic couplings replace the deterministic matching and produce
randomized insertion dynamics and interacting particle systems.


\subsection[latticeModelList]{Symmetric functions via lattice models}

In \cite{CurranFrechetteYostWolffZhangZhang2021x},
the authors construct a 6-vertex model for (supersymmetric) \hyperref[LLT]{LLT polynomials}
and they prove a Cauchy identity for the spin LLT polynomials $\LLTG^{(k)}_{\lambda/\mu}(\xvec;q)$.
The same model is considered in \cite{Hardt2021x}, and it is shown 
that the partition function is more or less unique in a certain sense.

For metaplectic \hyperref[whittaker]{Whittaker functions}, see
\cite{BrubakerBuciumasBumpGustafsson2020}.
For a broad introduction to the interplay between integrability and
combinatorics, see \cite{ZinnJustin2024x}.

For lattice models for \hyperref[grothendieck]{Grothendieck polynomials}, see
\cite{BrubakerFrechetteHardtTiborWeber2020x,BuciumasScrimshaw2020x}.

A 5-vertex lattice model for \hyperref[key]{Demazure character} is given in \cite{Yang2025x}.
A few years earlier, 5-vertex model for \hyperref[atom]{Demazure atoms}
 was described in \cite{BrubakerBuciumasBumpGustafsson2021}.

\name{Konstantin Matveev} constructs positivity-preserving operators from the
stochastic six-vertex model and applies them to Hall--Littlewood positivity
\cite{Matveev2023x}.  The same framework is related to $t$-deformed Schensted
insertions.

\name{Atsuo Kuniba}, \name{Masato Okado}, and \name{Travis Scrimshaw}
construct a $t$-oscillator weighted five-vertex model for the stationary
states of the multispecies ASEP on a ring \cite{KunibaOkadoScrimshaw2024x}.
The model gives a partition-function interpretation of the multiline queue
construction and clarifies its relation with corner transfer matrices.

In \cite{GunnaWheelerZinnJustin2025x}, the authors construct a lattice model for 
a new family of symmetric functions, which unifies
\hyperref[qWhittaker]{$q$-Whittaker polynomials}, inhomogeneous $q$-Whittaker polynomials,
\hyperref[grothendieck]{Grothendieck polynomials} and their duals.

\name{Amol Aggarwal}, \name{Alexei Borodin}, and \name{Michael Wheeler}
construct colored fermionic vertex models whose partition functions give
symmetric functions with Cauchy and branching identities
\cite{AggarwalBorodinWheeler2021x}.  Their specializations include LLT and
factorial LLT polynomials, while a cylindrical version gives partition
functions for symmetric and nonsymmetric Macdonald polynomials.

\name{Lisa Johnston}, \name{Evuilynn Nguyen}, and \name{Anne Schilling}
realize RSK and uncrowding directly in a five-vertex model and construct the
associated crystal structure \cite{JohnstonNguyenSchilling2026x}.  This
connects the vertex-model partition functions to tableaux and
Grothendieck-polynomial combinatorics.

\name{Alexandr Garbali}, \name{Jan de Gier}, \name{William Mead}, and
\name{Michael Wheeler} define symmetric functions from the stochastic
six-vertex model in half-space with integrable boundary weights
\cite{GarbaliGierMeadWheeler2024}.  Double-row commutation relations give a
skew Cauchy identity, and a degeneration gives a Pfaffian partition-function
formula generalizing \name{Greg Kuperberg}'s formula for symmetry classes of
alternating sign matrices.

\name{Amol Aggarwal}, \name{Alexei Borodin}, \name{Leonid Petrov}, and
\name{Michael Wheeler} define two families of symmetric rational functions as
partition functions of the fully inhomogeneous free-fermion six-vertex model
\cite{AggarwalBorodinPetrovWheeler2023}.  These functions generalize Schur,
factorial Schur, and supersymmetric Schur polynomials, and satisfy Cauchy-type
identities from the Yang--Baxter equation.
For Borodin's higher-spin deformation of Hall--Littlewood functions,
see \hyperref[spinHallLittlewoodRationalFunction]{spin Hall--Littlewood rational functions}.

\subsection[bessenrodtStanleyPolynomials]{Bessenrodt--Stanley polynomials and the octahedron recurrence}

Let $\lambda$ be a Young diagram in English notation, with a variable
$x_{ij}$ attached to each box. For $(a,b)\in\lambda$, let
$\lambda_{a,b}$ be its southeast subdiagram: the boxes with
$i\geq a$ and $j\geq b$.
The \defin{Bessenrodt--Stanley polynomials} are
\[
 p_{a,b}(\xvec)
 =\sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda_{a,b}}
   \prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda_{a,b}\setminus\mu}x_{ij},
\]
where $\mu$ ranges over Young subdiagrams anchored at $(a,b)$,
including the empty and full diagrams
\cite[Eq.~(4.5)]{DiFrancesco2015}.
These are box-weighted polynomials, not generally symmetric functions.

\name{Philippe Di Francesco} realizes them as specializations of
solutions of the \defin{octahedron recurrence}
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 T_{i,j,k+1}T_{i,j,k-1}
 ={}&T_{i+1,j,k}T_{i-1,j,k}\\
 &+T_{i,j+1,k}T_{i,j-1,k}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Here the variables lie on the parity sublattice $i+j+k$ odd,
with $i\geq1$ and boundary $T_{0,j,k}=1$.
The Young diagram specifies a stepped initial surface.
The resulting solutions are Laurent polynomials with nonnegative
integer coefficients in the initial data; the Bessenrodt--Stanley
specialization cancels their denominators
\cite[Thms.~2.2 and 2.4]{DiFrancesco2015}.
The path and dimer partition-function formulas
\cite[Thms.~4.2 and 4.4]{DiFrancesco2015}
give a lattice-model interpretation of these polynomials.
