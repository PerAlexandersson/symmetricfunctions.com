\section[ehrhart]{Ehrhart theory}

\defin{Ehrhart theory}\label{ehrhartTheory} studies the lattice points in
dilates of rational and lattice polytopes.  For a lattice polytope \(P\), the
basic counting function is
\[
  E_P(n)=|nP\cap \setZ^d|.
\]
This is the \hyperref[ehrhartPolynomialDefinition]{Ehrhart polynomial} of
\(P\).  Its generating function is the
\hyperref[ehrhartSeries]{Ehrhart series}, usually written in the form
\[
  \sum_{n\geq 0} E_P(n)t^n
  =
  \frac{h^*_P(t)}{(1-t)^{d+1}},
\]
where \(h^*_P(t)\) is the
\hyperref[hStarPolynomial]{\(h^*\)-polynomial}.  See
\cite{Stanley1993,Beck2007} and the \hyperref[ehrhartPolytopes]{polytope}
page for the standard definitions and basic theorems.

\begin{example}
For the unit interval \(P=[0,1]\),
\[
  E_P(n)=n+1,\qquad
  \sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)t^n=\frac{1}{(1-t)^2}.
\]
Thus \(h^*_P(t)=1\).  For the unit square \([0,1]^2\),
\[
  E_P(n)=(n+1)^2,\qquad
  \sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)^2t^n=\frac{1+t}{(1-t)^3},
\]
so \(h^*_P(t)=1+t\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
For the \(d\)-dimensional cube \([0,1]^d\), the \(h^*\)-polynomial is the
\hyperref[eulerianPolynomial]{Eulerian polynomial}:
\[
\begin{array}{c|l}
d & h^*_{[0,1]^d}(t)\\
\hline
1 & 1\\
2 & 1+t\\
3 & 1+4t+t^2\\
4 & 1+11t+11t^2+t^3.
\end{array}
\]
The coefficients form the Eulerian number triangle \oeis{A008292}.
\end{example}

\subsection[ehrhartOperations]{Operations on lattice polytopes}

\name{Feihu Liu} compares how eight standard polytope operations affect
Ehrhart and magic positivity, coefficient properties of $h^*$-polynomials,
and lattice-geometric properties \cite[Table~1]{Liu2026EhrhartOperations}.

Integral dilation preserves several of these properties.  If $P$ is a
$d$-dimensional lattice polytope and $r$ is a positive integer, then

\[
  E_{rP}(n)=E_P(rn),
  \qquad
  h^*_{rP}(t)
  =\sum_{n\geq 0}[z^{rn}]
  \left((1+z+\dotsb+z^{r-1})^{d+1}h^*_P(z)\right)t^n.
\]

Thus dilation preserves Ehrhart positivity.  It also preserves magic
positivity and, whenever $h^*_P$ has the corresponding property,
unimodality, log-concavity without internal zeros, ultra log-concavity in
both the dimension and actual-degree normalizations, and real-rootedness.
It need not preserve symmetry or $\gamma$-positivity.  Dilation also preserves
spanning, very ampleness, the integer decomposition property, and
unimodular, regular unimodular, and quadratic triangulations
\cite[Sec.~9]{Liu2026EhrhartOperations}.

The behavior of the other operations differs.  A unit-height lattice pyramid
satisfies $h^*_{\operatorname{pyr}(P)}=h^*_P$, and therefore preserves every
coefficient property of $h^*$, but can destroy Ehrhart and magic positivity.
Cartesian products preserve Ehrhart and magic positivity, log-concavity
without internal zeros, dimension-order ultra log-concavity, and
real-rootedness of $h^*$.  Unimodality can fail under products, while
symmetry and $\gamma$-positivity require the two factors to have equal codegree.
Standard lattice joins preserve real-rootedness, whereas free sums need not
\cite[Secs.~2--5]{Liu2026EhrhartOperations}.

\subsection[ehrhartReciprocityHub]{Reciprocity and duality}

\hyperref[ehrhartMacdonaldReciprocity]{Ehrhart--Macdonald reciprocity} relates
lattice points in \(nP\) to lattice points in the interior of \(nP\), by
evaluating the Ehrhart polynomial at negative integers.  For a
\(d\)-dimensional lattice polytope,
\[
  E_P(-n)=(-1)^d |nP^\circ\cap \setZ^d|.
\]
In terms of the Ehrhart series, palindromicity of the
\hyperref[hStarVector]{\(h^*\)-vector} is tied to the
\hyperref[gorenstein]{Gorenstein} condition.  This is one reason Ehrhart
theory often behaves like a discrete shadow of Poincaré duality.

\subsection[hStarInequalities]{Inequalities for \(h^*\)-vectors}

The \hyperref[hStarVector]{\(h^*\)-vector}
\[
  h^*(P)=(h^*_0,h^*_1,\dotsc,h^*_d)
\]
of a \(d\)-dimensional lattice polytope is not an arbitrary nonnegative
integer vector.  The first restrictions are the basic ones:
\[
  h^*_0=1,\qquad
  h^*_1=|P\cap \setZ^d|-d-1,\qquad
  \sum_{i=0}^{d} h_i^*=\operatorname{Vol}(P),
\]
where \(\operatorname{Vol}(P)\) is the normalized volume.  If \(s\) is the
degree of \(h^*_P(t)\), then \(h^*_s\) counts lattice points in a suitable
interior dilation of \(P\), and in particular
\[
  h^*_d=|P^\circ\cap \setZ^d|.
\]
This gives the elementary inequality
\[
  h^*_d\leq h^*_1.
\]

There are also less immediate inequalities.  \name{Takayuki Hibi} proved
\cite{Hibi1994LowerBound} that
\[
  h^*_{d-1}+h^*_{d-2}+\dotsb+h^*_{d-i}
  \leq
  h^*_2+h^*_3+\dotsb+h^*_{i+1},
  \qquad 1\leq i\leq d-1.
\]
If \(P\) has an interior lattice point, equivalently \(h^*_d>0\), then Hibi
also proved
\[
  h^*_1\leq h^*_i,\qquad 1\leq i\leq d-1.
\]
Another family, due to \name{Richard P. Stanley}
\cite{Stanley1991HilbertFunction}, says that if \(s=\deg h^*_P(t)\), then
\[
  h^*_0+h^*_1+\dotsb+h^*_i
  \leq
  h^*_s+h^*_{s-1}+\dotsb+h^*_{s-i},
  \qquad 0\leq i\leq s.
\]

The two-dimensional case has a particularly concrete form.  Scott's theorem
\cite{Scott1976ConvexLatticePolygons} characterizes the possible
\(h^*\)-polynomials \(1+h^*_1t+h^*_2t^2\) of lattice polygons by the
following alternatives:
\[
  h^*_2=0,\qquad
  h^*_2\leq h^*_1\leq 3h^*_2+3,\qquad
  \text{or}\qquad (h^*_1,h^*_2)=(7,1).
\]
\name{Jaron Treutlein} extended the necessary part of this statement to
lattice polytopes of degree at most two \cite{Treutlein2010DegreeTwo}.

Finally, \name{Gabriele Balletti} and \name{Akihiro Higashitani}
\cite{BallettiHigashitani2018Universal} proved a universal version of Scott's
inequality.  If \(P\) is any lattice polytope, in any
dimension and of any degree, and
\[
  h^*_P(t)=1+h^*_1t+h^*_2t^2+\dotsb
  \qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad h^*_3=0,
\]
then one of the following holds:
\[
  h^*_2=0,\qquad
  h^*_1\leq 3h^*_2+3,\qquad
  \text{or}\qquad (h^*_1,h^*_2)=(7,1).
\]
The hypothesis \(h^*_3=0\) is essential: their paper gives a
five-dimensional example with \(h^*_P(t)=1+8t+t^2+8t^3\), which violates
Scott's inequality once the \(h^*_3\)-term is present.  Stapledon's work
\cite{Stapledon2009Inequalities,Stapledon2016Additive} gives further
families of inequalities, often sharper but less uniform in appearance.

\subsection[cyclicPolytopeMagicPositivity]{Cyclic polytopes and magic positivity}

Let
\[
  C_d(T)=\operatorname{conv}\{(t,t^2,\dotsc,t^d):t\in T\}
\]
be the \defin{integral cyclic polytope}
\label{integralCyclicPolytope} determined by a finite set $T\subset\setZ$.
\name{Masato Konoike} expresses its Ehrhart polynomial as the average of the
Ehrhart polynomials of an explicit family of lattice zonotopes
\cite[Thm.~1.1]{Konoike2026x}.  Since lattice zonotopes are
\hyperref[magicPositivePolynomial]{magic positive}, so is $E_{C_d(T)}(x)$.
Consequently, $h^*_{C_d(T)}(x)$ is
\hyperref[realRootedPolynomial]{real-rooted}, and its coefficients are
\hyperref[logConcavePolynomial]{log-concave} and
\hyperref[unimodalPolynomial]{unimodal} \cite[Cor.~1.2]{Konoike2026x}.

\subsection[ehrhartPosets]{Order polytopes and posets}

For a finite \hyperref[poset]{poset} \(P\), the
\hyperref[orderPolytope]{order polytope} \(\mathcal O(P)\) packages
order-preserving maps \(P\to[0,1]\).  Its \(h^*\)-polynomial is the
\hyperref[pEulerianPolynomial]{\(P\)-Eulerian polynomial}; equivalently, it
records descents of linear extensions.  The
\hyperref[chainPolytope]{chain polytope} \(\mathcal C(P)\) has the same
Ehrhart polynomial as \(\mathcal O(P)\), but its inequalities are controlled
by chains rather than order relations.

A \defin{fence poset}\label{fencePoset} is a poset on
\(z_1,z_2,\dotsc,z_n\) whose Hasse diagram is the path
\(z_1 - z_2 - \dotsb - z_n\), so that each consecutive pair satisfies either
\(z_{i-1}<z_i\) or \(z_{i-1}>z_i\).  Call \(z_1\) and every \(z_i\) with
\(z_{i-1}<z_i\) an \emph{ascent element}, and every \(z_i\) with
\(z_{i-1}>z_i\) a \emph{descent element}.
\name{Luis Ferroni}, \name{Alejandro H. Morales} and \name{Greta Panova}
proved that the \hyperref[orderPolynomial]{order polynomial}
\(\Omega_P(t)\) of every fence has nonnegative coefficients
\cite{FerroniMoralesPanova2026}; recall that
\(E_{\mathcal O(P)}(t)=\Omega_P(t+1)\).  The coefficients of
\(n!\,\Omega_P(t)\) are integers, and a combinatorial interpretation of them
was left open.  \name{Yakob Kahane} gives one through a permutation statistic
\cite{Kahane2026x}.
Let \(\sigma\colon P\to[n]\) be a bijection.  Read \(z_1,\dotsc,z_n\) from
left to right.  The element \(z_1\) is the root of the first block.  An ascent
element \(z_i\) starts a new block, of which it is the root, if
\(\sigma(z_i)\) is larger than the label of the root of the current block.
A descent element \(z_i\) starts a new block if \(\sigma(z_i)\) is smaller
than that label.  Otherwise \(z_i\) joins the current block.  Let
\(\operatorname{bl}_P(\sigma)\) be the number of blocks.

\begin{theorem}[{\cite[Thm.~3.8]{Kahane2026x}}]
If \(P\) is a fence poset on \(n\) elements, then
\[
  n!\,\Omega_P(t)=\sum_{\sigma}t^{\operatorname{bl}_P(\sigma)},
\]
where the sum is over all \(n!\) bijections \(\sigma\colon P\to[n]\).
\end{theorem}

When \(P\) is a chain, the blocks start at the left-to-right maxima of
\(\sigma\), and this is the classical identity
\(t(t+1)\dotsm(t+n-1)=\sum_{\sigma\in\symS_n}t^{\operatorname{cyc}(\sigma)}\)
via the Foata correspondence.
Since the coefficient of \(t^j\) in \(\Omega_P(t)\) is the probability that a
uniformly random labelling has \(j\) blocks, the coefficients of
\(E_{\mathcal O(P)}(t)=\sum_k i_k(P)t^k\) are expectations,
\(i_k(P)=\mathbb{E}\binom{\operatorname{bl}_P}{k}\)
\cite[Lem.~5.1]{Kahane2026x}.  In particular, if the maximal monotone runs
of the fence contain \(u_1,\dotsc,u_r\) cover relations, then
\[
  i_1(P)\geq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\dotsb+\frac{1}{u_j+1}\right),
\]
with equality if and only if \(P\) is a chain \cite[Thm.~5.3]{Kahane2026x}.
Kahane conjectures analogous block statistics for circular fences and for
skew-shape posets \cite[Conj.~4.3 and Conj.~4.7]{Kahane2026x}.

\begin{example}
Let \(P\) be the fence \(z_1<z_2<z_3>z_4\).  For the labelling
\((\sigma(z_1),\sigma(z_2),\sigma(z_3),\sigma(z_4))=(2,4,1,3)\), the blocks are
\(\{z_1\}\), \(\{z_2,z_3\}\) and \(\{z_4\}\), so
\(\operatorname{bl}_P(\sigma)=3\).  The only labellings with a single block are
\((3,1,2,4)\) and \((3,2,1,4)\).  Summing over all \(24\) labellings gives
\[
  24\,\Omega_P(t)=3t^4+10t^3+9t^2+2t=t(t+1)(t+2)(3t+1),
\]
and
\(E_{\mathcal O(P)}(t)=\tfrac18t^4+\tfrac{11}{12}t^3+\tfrac{19}{8}t^2+\tfrac{31}{12}t+1\).
The runs have \(u_1=2\) and \(u_2=1\) cover relations, and indeed
\(i_1(P)=\tfrac{31}{12}\geq 1+\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac13\right)+\tfrac12=\tfrac73\).
\end{example}
% Checked by brute force for all fences with at most 7 elements (Thm. 3.8, Lem. 5.1, Thm. 5.3).

Fences also control Ehrhart polynomials of
\hyperref[matroidBasePolytope]{matroid base polytopes}.
Ferroni, Morales and Panova write the Ehrhart polynomial of the base polytope
of a \hyperref[latticePathMatroidDefinition]{lattice path matroid} as a sum of
order polynomials of fences \cite{FerroniMoralesPanova2026x}.  Kahane
regroups this sum so that there is one term, the order polynomial of a poset
\(\operatorname{sh}(x)\), for each basis \(x\):
\[
  E_{\mathcal P(M)}(t)=\sum_{x}\Omega_{\operatorname{sh}(x)}(t)
\]
\cite[Cor.~6.5]{Kahane2026x}.  For a
\hyperref[schubertMatroidDefinition]{Schubert matroid} on \([n]\), combining
this with the theorem above writes \(n!\,E_{\mathcal P(M)}(t)\) as a sum of
\(t^{\operatorname{TrBl}(\sigma)}\) over pairs of a basis \(x\) and a
permutation \(\sigma\in\symS_n\), where \(\operatorname{TrBl}\) is the block
statistic of a truncation of a fence attached to \(x\)
\cite[Thm.~6.8]{Kahane2026x}.  So the coefficients of
\(n!\,E_{\mathcal P(M)}(t)\) count such pairs.  For the uniform matroid this
gives a combinatorial interpretation of the Ehrhart coefficients of the
\hyperref[hypersimplex]{hypersimplex} \(\Delta_{k,n}\)
\cite[Prop.~6.9]{Kahane2026x}, which Kahane presents as an answer to a
question of \name{Richard P. Stanley}.

\begin{example}
The hypersimplex \(\Delta_{2,4}\) is the base polytope of the lattice path
matroid of the \(2\times 2\) square.  Its six bases correspond to the
partitions \(\nu\subseteq(2,2)\), and the fences in the sum are the outer rims
of these \(\nu\): the empty poset, one element, a two-element chain (twice),
the three-element poset with a unique minimum, and the three-element poset
with a unique maximum.  Hence
\[
  E_{\Delta_{2,4}}(t)
  =1+t+2\binom{t+1}{2}+2\cdot\frac{t(t+1)(2t+1)}{6}
  =\frac23t^3+2t^2+\frac73t+1.
\]
\end{example}
% Checked: lattice-point counts of t*Delta_{2,4} and of all connected skew
% shapes in a 3x3 box agree with the sum over rims of nu/mu.

\begin{example}
If \(P\) is an antichain on \(d\) elements, then
\(\mathcal O(P)=[0,1]^d\).  Hence
\[
  E_{\mathcal O(P)}(n)=(n+1)^d.
\]
Its \(h^*\)-polynomial is the
\hyperref[eulerianPolynomial]{Eulerian polynomial}, whose coefficients are
the Eulerian numbers \oeis{A008292}.

If \(P\) is a chain on \(d\) elements, then
\(\mathcal O(P)\) is a simplex, and \(E_{\mathcal O(P)}(n)\) counts weakly
increasing sequences
\[
  0\leq a_1\leq a_2\leq \dotsb \leq a_d\leq n,
\]
so \(E_{\mathcal O(P)}(n)=\binom{n+d}{d}\) and \(h^*_{\mathcal O(P)}(t)=1\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Let \(P_\lambda\) be the Ferrers poset of a partition \(\lambda\).  Linear
extensions of \(P_\lambda\) are
\hyperref[standardYoungTableau]{standard Young tableaux} of shape \(\lambda\),
and the \hyperref[hStarPolynomial]{\(h^*\)-polynomial} of
\(\mathcal O(P_\lambda)\) is the descent
generating polynomial over those tableaux.  For \(\lambda=(n,n)\), this gives
the Narayana polynomial; the Narayana triangle is \oeis{A001263}.  This is
the Ehrhart-theoretic form of the example on the
\hyperref[realRootedTableaux]{real-rooted tableaux page}.
\end{example}

\subsection[ehrhartWhereItAppears]{Where it appears}

Ehrhart theory appears in several parts of algebraic combinatorics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \hyperref[gtpolytopes]{Gelfand--Tsetlin polytopes} and marked order
polytopes model representation-theoretic multiplicities.
\item The leading coefficient ratios of the
\hyperref[birkhoffPolytopeAsGelfandTsetlin]{Birkhoff-polytope Ehrhart
polynomial} satisfy Stanley's asymptotic formula
\cite{LiuTang2026Birkhoff}.
\item \hyperref[gtKostkaEhrhart]{Skew and flagged GT-polytopes} give
stretched Kostka, flagged skew Schur, and Littlewood--Richardson-type
counting functions.
\item \hyperref[littlewoodRichardsonModels]{Littlewood--Richardson
coefficients} can be studied through lattice points in hive and related
polytopes.
\item \hyperref[matroidBasePolytope]{Matroid base polytopes} and
\hyperref[positroidPolytopes]{positroid polytopes} have Ehrhart theory tied
to matroid invariants.
\item \hyperref[gammaPositivity]{Gamma-positivity} and
\hyperref[realRootedTableaux]{real-rootedness questions} often ask whether
the corresponding \(h^*\)-polynomials have stronger positivity properties.
\item The \hyperref[transferMatrixMethod]{transfer matrix method} can compute
Ehrhart series when lattice points are built from finitely many boundary
states.
\end{itemize}

\subsection[ehrhartConjecturalPositivity]{Ehrhart positivity: results and conjectures}

\hyperref[lectureHallSimplex]{Lecture hall simplices} need not be Ehrhart
positive, even when they admit regular flag unimodular triangulations.
For $\mathbf{s}=(a,\dotsc,a,a+1)$ of length $n\geq5$, the coefficient
of $t^{n-4}$ is negative for all sufficiently large $a$
\cite[Thm.~3.5 and Cor.~4.16]{CaicedoJuhnkePoullot2025x}.

\name{Luis Ferroni} and \name{Daniel McGinnis} prove Ehrhart positivity for
\(\{x\in[0,c_1]\times\dotsb\times[0,c_n]:x_1+\dotsb+x_n=k\}\),
where the \(c_i\) are positive integers and \(k\) is an integer with
\(0<k<c_1+\dotsb+c_n\) \cite[Thm.~1.1]{FerroniMcGinnis2025}.
These slices generalize \hyperref[hypersimplex]{hypersimplices}.
Their result also gives a short proof of Ehrhart positivity for independence
polytopes of uniform matroids \cite[Cor.~6.4]{FerroniMcGinnis2025}.

\name{Feihu Liu}, \name{Jinlong Tang}, \name{Sihao Tao}, and
\name{Zihao Zhang} determine the smallest number of facets of a smooth
$d$-dimensional lattice polytope with a negative Ehrhart coefficient.  The
minimum is $6$ for $d=3$, $7$ for $d=4$, and $d+2$ for every $d\geq5$;
no such polytope exists in dimensions $1$ and $2$
\cite[Thm.~1.1]{LiuTangTaoZhang2026x}.

The next theorem rests on the
\hyperref[markedOrderPolytopePositivity]{ideal-chain decomposition for marked
order polytopes}, although not on its ideal/filter-closure criterion: the
relevant ideals and filters of the GT poset are shifted skew shapes, whose
Ehrhart positivity is not known in general \cite[Sec.~1]{JochemkoMenon2026x}.

\begin{theorem}[Jochemko--Menon {\cite[Thm.~3.5]{JochemkoMenon2026x}}]
The Ehrhart polynomial of the skew Gelfand--Tsetlin polytope
\(\gtp_{\lambda/\mu}\) has nonnegative coefficients.  Equivalently,
\[
  k\mapsto \schurS_{k\lambda/k\mu}(1^m)
\]
has nonnegative coefficients as a polynomial in \(k\).
\name{Katharina Jochemko} and \name{Krishna Menon} thereby prove the
\hyperref[skewGTEhrhartPositivity]{Alexandersson--Alhajjar conjecture}
\cite[Conj.~7]{AlexanderssonAlhajjar2018x}.
\end{theorem}

Their method also proves Ehrhart positivity for the row-interval flagged
faces of skew GT polytopes \cite[Sec.~3.2]{JochemkoMenon2026x}.  Fixing the
tableau content as well, which intersects such a face with a rational affine
weight slice, leads to the open conjectures below.

The King--Tollu--Toumazet conjecture predicts that every coefficient of the
stretched Littlewood--Richardson polynomial
\[
  t\mapsto c^{t\nu}_{t\lambda,t\mu}
\]
is nonnegative; see the
\hyperref[kingTolluToumazetConjecture]{conjecture and its proved cases}.
It is known when the indexing partitions have at most five parts.

\begin{theorem}[Ferudun {\cite[Thms.~2--3]{Ferudun2026x}}]
If \(\lambda,\mu,\nu\) have length at most five, then every coefficient of
\(t\mapsto c^{t\nu}_{t\lambda,t\mu}\) is nonnegative.  Equivalently, the
Ehrhart polynomial of the associated hive polytope is coefficientwise
nonnegative.
\end{theorem}
For at most four parts the proof is structural, while the case of five parts
uses a computer-generated certificate.

\name{Per Alexandersson} and \name{Ezgi Kantarcı Oğuz} conjecture that the
flagged skew Schur stretching polynomials
$k\mapsto K_{k\lambda/k\mu,k\nu}(\avec,\bvec)$ have nonnegative
coefficients \cite{AlexanderssonOguz2023x}; see the
\hyperref[flaggedSchurKostkaConjecture]{flagged conjecture}.
\name{Per Alexandersson} and \name{Elie Alhajjar} conjecture that
$k\mapsto\key_{k\lambda,\sigma}(1^n)$ has nonnegative coefficients, also in
partial-sum variables \cite{AlexanderssonAlhajjar2018}; see the
\hyperref[keyEhrhartConjecture]{key-polynomial conjecture}.

\subsection[ehrhartQuestions]{Typical questions}

Common questions include:
\begin{itemize}
\item whether the coefficients of \(E_P(n)\) are nonnegative;
\item whether \(h^*_P(t)\) is unimodal, real-rooted, or
\hyperref[gammaPositivePolynomial]{gamma-positive};
\item whether a rational polytope has
\hyperref[periodCollapse]{period collapse};
\item whether a family admits a combinatorial interpretation for its
\(h^*\)-coefficients.
\end{itemize}

These questions are often subtle: the \(h^*\)-vector of a lattice polytope is
always nonnegative, but Ehrhart polynomial coefficients can be negative.
