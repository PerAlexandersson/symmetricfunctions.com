The \defin{monomial slide polynomials}\label{monomialSlidePolynomialDefinition}
were introduced by \name{Sami Assaf} and \name{Dominic Searles}
in \cite{AssafSearles2016}.
The slide polynomials form a basis for the space of polynomials, and can be seen as a lift
of the \hyperref[qmonom]{monomial quasisymmetric functions}.
Together with the \hyperref[slideF]{fundamental slide polynomials}, they give
finite-variable lifts of the two basic bases of
\hyperref[quasiSymmetricFunctions]{quasisymmetric functions}.  This page also
records nearby nonsymmetric families such as glide, forest, and lock
polynomials.


\begin{definition}
For a \hyperref[weakCompositionDefinition]{weak composition} $\alpha$, set
\[
\slideM_\alpha(\xvec)
\coloneqq
\sum_{\substack{b \trianglerighteq \alpha \\
                \mathrm{flat}(b)=\mathrm{flat}(\alpha)}}
\xvec^b
\]
where $\mathrm{flat}(\beta)$ is the
\defin{flat composition}\label{flatComposition} obtained by removing all 0s
from $\beta$, and $\trianglerighteq$ denotes
\hyperref[dominanceOrder]{dominance order}.
\end{definition}

As an example (from \cite[Eq.~3.7]{AssafSearles2016}),
\[
\slideM_{(0,2,0,3)}(\xvec)
=
x_1^2x_2^3 + x_1^2x_3^3 + x_1^2 x_4^3
+ x_2^2 x_3^3  + x_2^2 x_4^3.
\]



The monomial slide polynomials have the \hyperref[qmonom]{monomial quasisymmetric functions}
as stable limit:
\[
 \lim_{m \to \infty} \slideM_{0^m\times \alpha}(\xvec) = \qmonom_{\mathrm{flat}(\alpha)}(\xvec).
\]

The \defin{fundamental slide polynomials}\label{fundamentalSlidePolynomialDefinition}
(or sometimes just \defin{slide polynomials})
were introduced by \name{Sami Assaf} and \name{Dominic Searles} in \cite{AssafSearles2016}.
The slide polynomials form a basis for the space of polynomials, and can be seen as a lift
of the \hyperref[gessel]{Gessel quasisymmetric functions}.
\name{Oliver Pechenik} and \name{Dominic Searles} give a survey of
asymmetric function theory \cite{PechenikSearles2020}.  It is a useful entry
point for slide, glide, key, Demazure atom, and related nonsymmetric or
quasisymmetric families.

The $K$-theoretic analogue of fundamental slide polynomials is given by the
\defin{glide polynomials}\label{glidePolynomial}
\cite{PechenikSearles2017}.
They refine \hyperref[grothendieck]{Grothendieck polynomials} in the
same way that fundamental slide polynomials refine
\hyperref[schubert]{Schubert polynomials}.
A definition and a small example are given after the fundamental slide
example below.


The fundamental slide polynomials expand positively 
in the \hyperref[slideM]{monomial slide polynomials}.
Moreover, the \hyperref[schubert]{Schubert polynomials}
expand positively in the fundamental slide polynomials
\cite[Thm.~3.13]{AssafSearles2016}.
The main motivation for introducing the fundamental slide polynomials,
is that products of Schubert polynomials can be expanded, with a combinatorial
formula, into fundamental slide polynomials.

\begin{definition}
For a \hyperref[weakCompositionDefinition]{weak composition} $\alpha$, the
\hyperref[fundamentalSlidePolynomialDefinition]{fundamental slide polynomial}
$\slideF_\alpha$ is defined as
\[
\slideF_\alpha(\xvec)
\coloneqq
\sum_{\substack{b \trianglerighteq \alpha \\
                \mathrm{flat}(b)
                \text{ refines }
                \mathrm{flat}(\alpha)}}
\xvec^b
\]
where refinement is the
\hyperref[compositionRefinementOrder]{composition refinement order}, and
$\trianglerighteq$ denotes \hyperref[dominanceOrder]{dominance order}.
\end{definition}


The fundamental slide polynomials have the \hyperref[gessel]{Gessel quasisymmetric functions}
as stable limit:
\[
 \lim_{m \to \infty} \slideF_{0^m\times \alpha}(\xvec) = \gessel_{\mathrm{flat}(\alpha)}(\xvec).
\]

\begin{example}
% Related Rust: sym-poly/multipoly/src/slide_polynomial.rs and
% sym-poly/multipoly/examples/slide_site_example.rs
For $\alpha=(1,0,2)$, the monomial slide polynomial is
\[
\slideM_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)
=
x_1x_3^2+x_1x_2^2.
\]
The support can be seen directly from the weak-composition diagrams.  We record
the same information by listing the rows from top to bottom: row $3$, row $2$,
then row $1$.
\[
\begin{aligned}
b=(1,0,2)&:\quad
(\bullet\bullet,\ \emptyset,\ \bullet)
  &&\longmapsto x_1x_3^2,\\
b=(1,1,1)&:\quad
(\bullet,\ \bullet,\ \bullet)
  &&\longmapsto x_1x_2x_3,\\
b=(1,2,0)&:\quad
(\emptyset,\ \bullet\bullet,\ \bullet)
  &&\longmapsto x_1x_2^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first and last diagrams have flat composition $(1,2)$, so they contribute
to $\slideM_{(1,0,2)}$.  The middle diagram has flat composition $(1,1,1)$,
which refines $(1,2)$ but is not equal to it.
The fundamental slide polynomial permits the refinement $(1,1,1)$ of
$\mathrm{flat}(\alpha)=(1,2)$, so
\[
\slideF_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)
=
x_1x_3^2+x_1x_2x_3+x_1x_2^2
=
\slideM_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)+\slideM_{(1,1,1)}(\xvec).
\]
\end{example}

\name{Oliver Pechenik} and \name{Dominic Searles} define the glide
polynomials using weak compositions with coloured entries.
A \defin{weak komposition}\label{weakKomposition} (the spelling is theirs) is
a weak composition whose positive entries are each coloured black or red.
The \defin{excess} $\mathrm{ex}(b)$ of a weak komposition $b$ is its number
of red entries.

\begin{definition}
Let $\alpha$ be a weak composition of length $n$ with nonzero entries in
positions $n_1<\dotsb<n_\ell$.  A weak komposition $b$ of length $n$ is a
\defin{glide}\label{glideOfWeakComposition} of $\alpha$ if there are integers
$0=i_0<i_1<\dotsb<i_\ell$, with $b_k=0$ for $k>i_\ell$, such that for every
$1\leq j\leq \ell$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\alpha_{n_j}=b_{i_{j-1}+1}+\dotsb+b_{i_j}
  -\mathrm{ex}(b_{i_{j-1}+1},\dotsc,b_{i_j})$,
\item $i_j\leq n_j$, and
\item the leftmost nonzero entry among $b_{i_{j-1}+1},\dotsc,b_{i_j}$ is black.
\end{itemize}
The \hyperref[glidePolynomial]{glide polynomial} is
\[
\mathcal{G}^{(\beta)}_\alpha(\xvec)
\coloneqq
\sum_{b} \beta^{\mathrm{ex}(b)}\xvec^{b},
\]
where the sum is over all glides $b$ of $\alpha$
\cite[Def.~2.2 and Def.~2.5]{PechenikSearles2017}.
Different colourings of the same weak composition count as different glides.
\end{definition}

The third condition is stated for the leftmost \emph{nonzero} entry in
\cite[Def.~2.1]{MonicalPechenikSearles2021}, which restates the definition
of \cite{PechenikSearles2017}.
Setting $\beta=0$ keeps only the glides without red entries, and
$\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_\alpha=\slideF_\alpha$
\cite[Prop.~2.16]{PechenikSearles2017}.

\begin{example}
Let $\alpha=(1,0,2)$, so $n_1=1$ and $n_2=3$.
The first block is $b_1$ alone.  Its only nonzero entry must be black, so
$b_1=1$.
The second block is $b_2$ or $b_2b_3$, and its sum is $2$ plus its number of
red entries.
Without red entries we get $(1,0,2)$, $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,2,0)$, the three
exponents of $\slideF_{(1,0,2)}$ above.
A red entry must be $b_3$, with $b_2\geq 1$ black.  This gives the two glides
$(1,1,2)$ and $(1,2,1)$ with $b_3$ red.  Two red entries are impossible.
Hence
\[
\mathcal{G}^{(\beta)}_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)
=
x_1x_3^2+x_1x_2x_3+x_1x_2^2
+\beta\left(x_1x_2x_3^2+x_1x_2^2x_3\right),
\]
and setting $\beta=0$ recovers $\slideF_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)$.
\end{example}

\name{Dominic Searles} introduced the
\defin{fundamental particle basis}\label{fundamentalParticleBasis}
\cite{Searles2019PolynomialBases}.  \name{Angela Hicks} and \name{Elizabeth Niese}
identify this basis with the fundamental Demazure atoms obtained by replacing
the usual Demazure atom operators with Hivert's quasisymmetric divided
difference operators \cite{HicksNiese2024x}.  In this form the basis has an
explicit monomial-positive formula, and the fundamental slide polynomials
expand positively in the fundamental particle basis.

Searles defines the basis through local moves on weak compositions.
A \defin{slide}\label{slideOfWeakComposition} of $\alpha$ is a weak
composition obtained from $\alpha$ by a sequence of moves
$\dotsc 0k\dotsc \to \dotsc ij\dotsc$ with $i+j=k$ and $i,j\geq 0$.
It is a \defin{fixed slide}\label{fixedSlide} if, in addition, $j>0$
whenever $k$ occupies a position where $\alpha$ is nonzero.
The fundamental slide polynomial $\slideF_\alpha$ is the generating
function of the slides of $\alpha$, and
\[
L_\alpha(\xvec) \coloneqq \sum_{b \text{ fixed slide of } \alpha} \xvec^{b}
\]
\cite[Def.~4.1, Lem.~4.3 and Def.~4.4]{Searles2019PolynomialBases}.
The fundamental slide polynomials expand into fundamental particles by the
same rule that expands monomial slide polynomials into monomials:
\[
\slideF_\alpha(\xvec)
=
\sum_{\substack{b \trianglerighteq \alpha \\
                \mathrm{flat}(b)=\mathrm{flat}(\alpha)}}
L_b(\xvec)
\]
\cite[Prop.~4.7]{Searles2019PolynomialBases}.

\begin{example}
Let $\alpha=(1,0,2)$ as in the fundamental slide example above.
Its slides are $(1,0,2)$, $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,2,0)$.
Position $3$ is nonzero in $\alpha$, so a fixed slide may not move all of the
$2$ out of position $3$.  This excludes $(1,2,0)$, and
\[
L_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)=x_1x_3^2+x_1x_2x_3,
\qquad
L_{(1,2,0)}(\xvec)=x_1x_2^2.
\]
The weak compositions $b\trianglerighteq(1,0,2)$ with
$\mathrm{flat}(b)=(1,2)$ are $(1,0,2)$ and $(1,2,0)$, so
\[
\slideF_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)
=
L_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)+L_{(1,2,0)}(\xvec)
=
\slideM_{(1,0,2)}(\xvec)+\slideM_{(1,1,1)}(\xvec).
\]
Both expansions have two terms, but they group the monomial $x_1x_2x_3$
differently.  In the monomial slide expansion it forms
$\slideM_{(1,1,1)}$ on its own.  In the particle expansion it lies in
$L_{(1,0,2)}$, together with the leading monomial $x_1x_3^2$.
In the operator description of \cite{HicksNiese2024x}, this is
$L_{(1,0,2)}=\tilde{\theta}_2\, x_1x_2^2$, where Hivert's operator
$\tilde{\theta}_2$ sends $\xvec^{(a,c,0)}$ with $c>0$ to
$\sum_{j=1}^{c} \xvec^{(a,c-j,j)}$
\cite[Def.~3.1, Lem.~3.3, Lem.~3.4 and Cor.~3.11]{HicksNiese2024x}.
\end{example}


\subsection[slidePositive]{Slide positive families}

\hyperref[key]{Key polynomials} expand positively in the
fundamental slide basis \cite[Thm.~2.13]{AssafSearles2018}.
In \cite{ChoWilligenburg2022}, the authors determine for which $\alpha$,
the key polynomials $\key_\alpha$ expanded into slide polynomials are multiplicity free.

The \hyperref[pPartitionsFlagged]{flagged $(P,w)$-partition generating functions}
of \name{Sami Assaf} and \name{Nantel Bergeron} are slide-positive
\cite{AssafBergeron2019}.
In \cite{TewariWilsonZhang2022}, it is shown that certain polynomials similar to 
\hyperref[chromaticQuasisymmetric]{chromatic symmetric functions} are slide-positive.


See \cite{SmirnovTutubalina2021} for the notion of \emph{slide complexes}.

The \hyperref[kohnert]{Kohnert polynomial} page gives a diagrammatic model
which contains keys, Schubert polynomials, locks, and other Assaf--Searles
families as special cases.  For $K$-theoretic diagram models related to
Lascoux and glide phenomena, see the \hyperref[lascoux]{Lascoux polynomial}
page.

\name{Philippe Nadeau} and \name{Vasu Tewari}
\cite{NadeauTewari2023x} introduce
\defin{forest polynomials}\label{forestPolynomialDefinition}, indexed by
forests with labeled vertices.  These polynomials can be defined as generating functions for
labelings of forest posets with weak and strict inequalities along the two
types of edges.  This puts them close in spirit to flagged
\hyperref[pPartition]{P-partitions}.

Forest polynomials form a basis of the polynomial ring, and they expand
positively in the \hyperref[slideF]{fundamental slide basis}.  Conversely,
\hyperref[schubert]{Schubert polynomials} expand positively in the forest
polynomial basis.

\name{Philippe Nadeau}, \name{Hunter Spink}, and \name{Vasu Tewari}
\cite{NadeauSpinkTewari2024x} develop a quasisymmetric divided-difference
formalism in which forest polynomials play the role of Schubert polynomials.
Their trimming operators are indexed by forests and governed by the Thompson
monoid.  In this setting forest polynomials are characterized recursively by
the action of the trimming operators, and products of forest polynomials are
forest-positive.
\name{Annie Guo} and \name{Dora Woodruff} characterize when a Schubert
polynomial is itself a forest polynomial \cite{GuoWoodruff2026x}.  Their
criterion is that the indexing permutation avoids a specific set of six
patterns, giving another pattern-avoidance description of a special Schubert
class.
\name{Matthew J. Samuel} gives a Littlewood--Richardson rule for products of
forest polynomials using forest RC-graphs and a Schubert bialgebra
\cite{Samuel2026x}.  The same bialgebra framework yields product rules for
dual Schubert, key, and slide polynomials.

The \defin{lock polynomials}\label{lockPolynomialDefinition} were introduced by
\name{Sami Assaf} and \name{Dominic Searles} in \cite{AssafSearles2019}.
The lock polynomials form a basis for the polynomial ring,
and are indexed by \hyperref[weakCompositionDefinition]{weak compositions}.
The combinatorial formula for these is
\[
\lock_\alpha(\xvec) \coloneqq \sum_{T \in LT(\alpha)} \xvec^T
\]
where the sum is over all \defin{lock tableaux}\label{lockTableau}.

As an example (from \cite{Wang2020}), we have
\[
\lock_{(0,2,3)}(\xvec)
= x_2^2 x_3^2 + x_1x_2x_3^3 + x_1^2x_3^3 + x_1x_2^2x_3^2
+ x_1^2x_2x_3^2 + x_1^2x_2^2x_3 + x_1^2 x_2^3
\]

Whenever the nonzero parts of $\alpha$ are weakly decreasing,
the lock polynomial coincides with a \hyperref[key]{key polynomial};
$\lock_\alpha(\xvec)=\key_\alpha(\xvec)$, see
\cite[Thm.~6.12]{AssafSearles2019}.

There is a \hyperref[crystals]{crystal structure} on lock polynomials, 
explored by \name{George Wang} in \cite{Wang2020}.
This crystal structure embeds naturally into \hyperref[key]{Demazure crystals}.
